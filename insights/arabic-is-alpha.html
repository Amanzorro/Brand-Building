Arabic is alpha

Arabic-language disclosures and filings get less analyst coverage than English ones. That gap is an opportunity.

Nokhada6 min read

Ask a global analyst covering a GCC name where they get their information, and the answer is usually the English investor presentation, the English earnings release and a sell-side note, also in English. All useful. All read by every other analyst covering the name.

Ask where the primary information lives and the answer is often different. Many listed companies in the region publish material in Arabic first, and some publish certain items only in Arabic. Then there are commercial-registry entries, court judgments, municipal permits, real-estate transaction records, tender awards and regional press, much of it Arabic-only and almost none of it in a terminal's English news feed.

A gap in attention

This isn't hidden information. It's public and filed with exchanges or government bodies. It just gets less attention because reading it at scale has been expensive. A bilingual analyst can cover a handful of names properly, and machine translation helps less than it seems. Financial Arabic is formal and technical, and it often uses terms with no exact English equivalent. A clumsy translation of a footnote on contingent liabilities can easily be worse than none.

When reading something is expensive, few people read it. When few people read it, it's less likely to be priced in.

What changes when reading is cheap

Modern language models read Arabic well, with no translation step in between. Combined with a firm's own frameworks and a disciplined workflow, that turns a scarce skill into routine infrastructure. In practice:

  • Read both versions and compare them. Where a company publishes in both languages, the system reads both and flags material differences in emphasis, in detail or in what one version leaves out.
  • Watch the long tail. Tender awards, licensing decisions, court filings and land transactions relating to your holdings and watchlist are monitored and summarised daily.
  • Diligence without the translation queue. Arabic documents in a data room are read on the first pass alongside the English ones, rather than waiting a week for a translator.
  • Write for every reader. Outputs are produced in whichever language each reader prefers, in a house style set for that language rather than translated from English.

Two practical cautions

First, sources must be cited, always. A model that reads Arabic well can also summarise it confidently and wrongly. Every claim in our workflows links to the page it came from, so a bilingual reviewer can check it in seconds.

Second, the advantage depends on judgement. Reading more documents only helps if the system knows what matters to your process. That's why the firm's frameworks come first: they tell the system what to look for and what to ignore.

Global funds arriving in the Gulf bring capital and process. Local firms already understand the context. Covering Arabic sources at scale lets both get more out of what they have.

For firms based here, this is a natural advantage that was too costly to use. Now it isn't. Talk to us about putting it to work.
