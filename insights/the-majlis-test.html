The Majlis Test

Why every thesis should face a room built to sink it, and how we run that room with Claude Code before your committee meets.

Nokhada8 min read

In a traditional majlis, an idea is put to the room and the room responds. The elders don't simply nod. They ask who benefits, what happens if the rains fail and whether the proposer has thought about the neighbouring tribe. An idea that survives the majlis has been tested by people with every reason to find its weak point.

Most investment research skips this step. The process is built to assemble a case. The analyst has a view, gathers evidence, writes it up and, being a professional, adds a "risks" section near the end. That section is usually written by the same person who wrote the thesis, the night before the memo is due. It's there to show that risks were considered, not to test whether the thesis survives them.

Balanced research is usually just indecisive

The obvious fix is to "consider both sides", and that's often worse. A memo that sets out bull and bear cases in equal measure feels rigorous, but it hands the hard work, deciding which side wins, back to the committee with less time and less context than the analyst had. The usual result is a hedged conclusion and a smaller position than the evidence deserved, in either direction.

A better approach is to name the thesis plainly and then send something into the room whose only job is to sink it.

How the test works

We run the Majlis Test as a Claude Code workflow inside the client's environment. It has four steps.

  1. State the thesis as a single sentence. "The bank's margin expansion survives the next cutting cycle." "This logistics platform can double EBITDA without new capex." If the thesis can't be written as one sentence, it isn't ready.
  2. Break it into load-bearing assumptions. These are the three to six things that must all be true for the thesis to hold. Reading the firm's sector playbook and thesis archive first means the breakdown reflects what has actually decided outcomes in this sector before.
  3. Attack each assumption with specific evidence. This isn't general scepticism. Each assumption gets a targeted search for disconfirming evidence across filings, transcripts, data rooms, alternative data and Arabic-language sources an English-only process would miss. Every claim cites its source.
  4. Return a verdict per assumption, then overall. Each one comes back Holds or Sinks. If any load-bearing assumption sinks, so does the thesis.
There is no "inconclusive" verdict. If the evidence isn't enough to sink an assumption, it holds, and the investigator's notes say what further evidence would sink it.

Taking away the middle option is deliberate. It makes the system commit, and it makes the PM's job sharper: interrogate the verdict rather than reconcile two vague cases.

What happens to a sunk thesis

A sunk thesis isn't thrown away. It goes into the thesis archive with the assumption that failed and the evidence that sank it. Six months later, if the market changes or new information appears, the system can re-run the test and report whether the original objection still stands.

Over time the archive becomes one of the firm's most valuable assets: a written record of how it reasons, where it has been wrong and which objections turned out to matter. Each new Majlis Test reads it first.

Why it suits Gulf markets

Some of the most important evidence in GCC markets sits in places global research processes don't look: Arabic-only exchange disclosures, local court records, land-department transactions and regional press. Because the Majlis Test reads both languages as a matter of course, it often finds the objection a consensus thesis missed simply because nobody read the Arabic version.

The human stays in charge. The test gives the PM a sharper question to answer, and the PM still decides. Many sunk theses are overruled with good reason, and the overrule is recorded too.

Speed is a side effect

An analyst running this kind of adversarial review by hand might spend several days on one thesis, so in practice it's rarely done. Run as a workflow, it takes minutes, which means it can be applied to every thesis rather than only to the ones a senior partner happens to doubt. The value isn't faster research. It's a kind of research that wasn't practical before.

If you'd like to see it on one of your own theses, that's what the diagnostic is for.
