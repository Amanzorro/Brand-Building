# In the Gulf, heat is the balance-sheet risk

*Physical climate risk here is mostly a story about heat, cooling and water, punctuated by rare floods that standard models underweight.*

Global climate-risk frameworks were mostly written with temperate economies in mind, where the headline physical risks are river floods, storms and wildfire. Applied to the UAE without adjustment, they tend to produce reassuring results. There are few rivers, no hurricanes and little forest. A naive screen calls most assets low risk.

That reading misses how climate actually reaches a Gulf balance sheet.

## Heat comes through cooling

In the UAE, heat shows up mainly as energy demand. Cooling makes up a large share of building electricity use, and every rise in peak temperature pushes up load, cost and emissions together. For an asset owner, that hits operating expenses. For a tenant, it hits comfort and productivity. For a lender, it shows up in the asset's long-term value as efficiency standards tighten.

Heat also affects people and equipment. Outdoor-work restrictions in summer, derating of electrical and mechanical plant, and wear on roads, rail and solar installations all appear in operating costs long before they appear in a risk register.

## Water is the hidden dependency

Most drinking water in the UAE comes from desalination, which is energy-intensive and concentrated at the coast. Water and energy risk are effectively the same risk here. Any asset-level assessment that treats them separately will miss how they compound.

## Rare events still matter

In April 2024, the UAE recorded its heaviest rainfall since records began. Roads, basements, airports and supply chains were disrupted for days. Urban drainage designed for an arid climate met an event far outside its design basis.

Low-frequency, high-impact events like this are exactly what short historical records underweight. A credible risk view for the Gulf has to take them seriously, even though they're rare, because the built environment isn't designed for them.

> The question isn't whether an asset is exposed to climate. It's which way: through its energy bill, its water supply, its workforce or its basement.

## What a useful assessment looks like

- **Asset-level, not postcode-level.** Two towers on the same street can have very different exposure because of basement depth, façade or cooling system.
- **Operational as well as acute.** Cooling load, water dependency and heat-related downtime belong next to flood and coastal exposure.
- **Evidence-backed.** Every rating should cite the data behind it, including hazard layers, building documents and meter history, so lenders and insurers can check it.
- **Kept current.** A register updated when assets, hazard data or rules change is worth more than a polished report from two years ago.
